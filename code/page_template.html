When the ice came south

The great ice sheets of the Northern Hemisphere at {{areas.n_stages|int}} moments of the last 3.6 million years, from the reconstructions of Batchelor and colleagues (2019): how big they grew, how far south they reached, which of today's cities and countries lay beneath them, and the land that appeared when the sea fell.

Twenty-six thousand years ago a sheet of ice lay over almost all of Canada and the northern United States, and another over Scandinavia, the Baltic and much of Britain. It was not the first time. The ice has advanced and retreated many times over the past few million years, and some of those advances reached further south than the last one.

In 2019 Christine Batchelor and eight colleagues gathered the published evidence for where the Northern Hemisphere's ice sheets reached, slice by slice through the Quaternary, and drew a best estimate of the ice for each of {{areas.n_stages|int}} time slices, from the late Gauss Chron, 2.6 to 3.59 million years ago, to the Last Glacial Maximum around 26,500 years ago. They also drew a minimum and a maximum outline for every slice before the LGM. I took the best estimates and asked what they cover today. Move the slider to step through the slices, and click anywhere on the map to see which of them reached that spot and how far it was from the ice edge.

    02 · How bigThe last ice age was not the biggest

    The ice we know best, the Last Glacial Maximum, covered {{stages.lgm.area|m1}} million km² in Batchelor and colleagues' best estimate, about {{x.lgm_on_land_m|f1}} million of it on today's land and {{x.lgm_on_sea_m|f1}} million ({{stages.lgm.on_sea_pct|int}}%) on what is now sea floor, from Hudson Bay to the Barents Sea. That is only the {{areas.lgm_rank|int}}th largest of the {{areas.n_stages|int}} slices. The biggest was MIS 6, {{stages.mis6.age|raw}}: {{stages.mis6.area|m1}} million km², {{areas.mis6_over_lgm_pct|int}}% more than the LGM. MIS 12 ({{stages.mis12.area|m1}} million) and MIS 16 ({{stages.mis16.area|m1}} million) were close behind.

    The authors' own table of areas, which comes with the outlines, gives the same order for the seven largest slices, and its figures are within {{areas.max_abs_diff_pct|f1}}% of mine for every slice but one. The exception is 40 ka, where the outline holds {{areas.ka40_diff_pct|int}}% more ice than the table, mostly ice masses in north-east Asia. The minimum and maximum outlines show how uncertain the older slices are: for MIS 20 to 24 they run from {{stages.mis20.tab_min|m1}} to {{stages.mis20.tab_max|m1}} million km².

    Three slices are smaller by design. For 45 ka, MIS 5a and MIS 5c the authors drew the ice at the warmest moment of the slice, not its largest, and these are the three smallest outlines, none above {{x.warm_max_m|f1}} million km². Add every slice together and the ice reached {{areas.union|m1}} million km² at one time or another, {{areas.union_over_lgm_pct|int}}% more than the LGM alone. Only {{areas.every|m1}} million km², about half of it Greenland, was ice in every slice.

    03 · How far southTo the Ohio, the Dnieper and the Don

    In North America the ice of the Last Glacial Maximum reached {{south.lgm_na.lat|f1}}°N, near {{south.lgm_na.near|raw}}, Indiana. In MIS 6 and MIS 12 it went further, to {{south.na_min.lat|f1}}°N in southern Illinois, about {{south_extra.na_min_to_ohio_mouth_km|int}} km from where the Ohio River flows into the Mississippi. In Europe the LGM ice reached its lowest latitude on the floor of the Celtic Sea south of Ireland, at {{south.lgm_eu.lat|f1}}°N; on the mainland it got no further south than {{south_extra.lgm_eu_mainland_lat|f1}}°N. In MIS 6 a lobe pushed down the Dnieper valley in Ukraine to {{south.mis6_eu.lat|f1}}°N, near {{south.mis6_eu.near|raw}}, and in MIS 16 another reached {{stages.mis16.south.eu.lat|f1}}°N in the Don basin of southern Russia.

    Mountain glaciers are not in these outlines. The authors drew only ice masses thought to have covered more than 50,000 km², so the ice caps of the Alps, the Pyrenees and the Rockies' southern ranges are left out, and the southern limits here are those of the continental ice sheets.

    04 · What lay beneath{{places.lgm.n|int}} towns and cities on old ice

    London was never under the ice in any of these outlines, but in MIS 12, the Anglian glaciation of British geologists, the edge came within {{cities.London.mis12|int}} km of the centre.     05 · Land from the seaWhen the sea fell 120 metres

    The water in all that ice came from the sea. At the Last Glacial Maximum the oceans stood about 120 to 125 m lower than today, by the figures quoted on Wikipedia. On the ETOPO1 sea floor, a fall of 120 m uncovers {{exposed.m120.km2|m1}} million km², {{exposed.m120.pct_of_land|f1}}% more land than the world has now. {{exposed.m120.under_lgm_ice|m1}} million km² of that was itself under the ice, which leaves {{exposed.m120.ice_free|m1}} million km² of new dry land. A fall of only 60 m already uncovers {{exposed.half_share_pct|int}}% as much.

    The largest new lands were in the tropics. The Sahul shelf joined New Guinea and Tasmania to Australia ({{shelf.sahul.m120_ice_free|m1}} million km² of new land in my box), and Sundaland joined Sumatra, Java and Borneo to mainland Asia ({{shelf.sundaland.m120_ice_free|m1}} million). In the Arctic, the shelves of the Laptev, Kara and East Siberian seas added {{shelf.siberian.m120_ice_free|m1}} million, and Beringia, the Bering and Chukchi seas, {{shelf.beringia.m120_ice_free|m1}} million: along the meridian of the Bering Strait, dry land would have run unbroken for about {{bering.width_km|int}} km from north to south. In Europe, the southern North Sea and the Channel became Doggerland, about {{x.doggerland_free_k|int}},000 km² of ice-free land in my box; its northern part lay under the ice.

    On a grid this coarse, about 18 km, every one of the {{x.joins_n|int}} land bridges I tested is already made at 60 m: Britain to the continent, Ireland to Britain, Asia to North America, New Guinea and Tasmania to Australia, Sumatra, Java and Borneo to Asia, Sri Lanka to India, Taiwan and Sakhalin to the mainland. Narrow, deeper straits can hide below that resolution, so I leave out Japan and Newfoundland.

    06 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Ice. The best-estimate shapefiles from Batchelor et al. (2019), from the authors' repository on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/7jen3). The {{areas.n_stages|int}} time slices and their ages are the authors': the LGM (about 26,500 years ago), 30, 35, 40 and 45 ka, MIS 4 (58,000 to 72,000 years ago), MIS 5a to 5d (72,000 to 117,000), MIS 6 (132,000 to 190,000), MIS 8, 10, 12, 16, MIS 20 to 24 (790,000 to 928,000), the early Matuyama Chron (1.78 to 2.6 million years ago) and the late Gauss Chron (2.6 to 3.59 million). The files are in a polar Lambert equal-area projection, except the LGM, which is in latitude and longitude; I put everything in the equal-area projection, repaired invalid shapes, dissolved each slice to one outline and measured its area there. Two files, 45 ka and the late Gauss Chron, also carry today's glaciers worldwide, Antarctica included; I keep only the Northern Hemisphere parts.

    What the outlines are. The authors stress that their reconstructions are generalised and partly subjective, that they should not be used for local studies, and that some, MIS 8 and MIS 10 above all, are highly uncertain and indicate the amount of ice rather than where its edge lay. For slices without direct evidence they borrowed the ice of a slice with a similar climate, and they used templates for Greenland, Iceland, the North American Cordillera and north-east Asia. Each outline aims at the largest extent within its slice, which may not have been reached everywhere at once. A city's distance to the edge is therefore a guide to the scale, not a local fact.

    Places and distances. Countries are Natural Earth's (1:10m); their area under ice is measured in the same equal-area projection. Cities and towns are Natural Earth's populated places with their population figure (pop_max), which counts the built-up area; summing them is approximate. Distances to the ice edge are true distances on the WGS84 ellipsoid from the place to the nearest point of the outline. On the map, the distances are worked out in your browser from the generalised outlines and can differ by a few kilometres.

    Sea floor. ETOPO10, a 10 arc-minute version of NOAA's ETOPO1, with cell areas on the sphere. Land today is ground above sea level or inside Natural Earth's land; sea is water joined to the oceans (the Black Sea is kept, the Caspian and lakes are not). Depth is the bedrock surface, so floating ice shelves do not count as shallow sea. The ice outlines are drawn on today's coasts, and I do the same: I do not model the crust sinking under the ice or rising since, or the sediment laid down, so the lower sea levels are a simple flood fill in reverse. The shelf boxes are mine.
